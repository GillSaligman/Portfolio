FIRST Robotics Competition

Overview

FIRST Robotics Competition (FRC) is an international high school robotics competition where teams design, build, and program industrial-sized robots to compete in challenging, alliance-based games. Over my four years in FRC, I developed extensive skills in mechanical design, team leadership, and problem-solving under pressure.

As Team Captain and Mechanical Lead throughout high school, I was responsible for:

  • Leading a team of 30+ students in robot design and development
  • Managing project timelines and resource allocation during build season
  • Designing and implementing complex mechanical systems
  • Mentoring younger team members in engineering principles
  • Representing the team at competitions and community events

My FRC journey taught me the value of rapid prototyping, iterative design, and collaborative problem-solving - skills that have proven invaluable in my engineering career. Through FRC, I discovered my passion for robotics and mechanical design while learning to lead teams through complex technical challenges.

2020 Season: Infinite Recharge

2020
Innovation Under Pressure

In my junior year as Team Captain and Mechanical Lead, we faced the challenge of designing a robot for Infinite Recharge, a game focused on scoring dodgeballs into goals. Our initial design featured a tank drive with a 360° turret for rapid aiming. However, after observing the first week of competition, I recognized significant design flaws that would hinder our competitive performance.

With our seniors away on a trip, I proposed a complete redesign. The team trusted my vision, and we completely rebuilt the robot in just one week. The new design featured a "washing machine" carousel spinner with a hollow center tube and an omni wheel feeding mechanism. This innovation increased our scoring rate from one ball every 3-4 seconds to five balls in the same timeframe.

Tragically, the competition was canceled due to the COVID-19 pandemic, but this season remains a testament to our team's resilience and ability to innovate under pressure.

Turret-Based Scoring System

Our initial design featured a tank drive with a 360° turret for rapid aiming. This approach complemented the tank drive's maneuverability limitations by allowing independent aiming while moving.

The system included a flywheel for consistent ball velocity, a vertically adjustable hood for trajectory control, and a unique feeding mechanism. To ensure consistent feeding regardless of turret orientation, I designed a pulley system that achieved 6 inches of throw from a 2-inch piston.

Challenges included maintaining a low profile for field navigation, which created feeding issues. I solved this with a custom piston extender that doubled the throw without increasing height. Wire management was critical with the flywheel near sensitive electronics.

The "Washing Machine" Design

After observing gameplay during the first competition week, I recognized that our piston-based feeding mechanism was too slow for competitive play. With the seniors away, I led the team through a complete redesign.

The new system featured a rotating "washing machine" carousel with a hollow center tube. An omni wheel positioned above a cutout in the tube would spin to push balls into the center while allowing them to pass when not engaged.

This innovative approach increased our scoring rate dramatically - from one ball every 3-4 seconds to five balls in the same timeframe. The redesign was completed in just one week, demonstrating our team's ability to innovate rapidly under pressure.

Continuing a Legacy

Sam Ozer was a beloved member of our team and community whose life was tragically cut short in a biking accident during our build season. Sam had been developing an innovative climbing mechanism that would have given our robot a competitive edge.

His ingenious design used two opposed tape measures to launch a hook upward with Kevlar rope. The opposed configuration provided stability that a single tape measure couldn't achieve. After the competition cancellation, I took it upon myself to complete Sam's work as a tribute to his memory and ingenuity.

Using Sam's laser-cut prototypes as a foundation, I engineered a metal version capable of handling the required forces. This project was more than technical problem-solving - it was a way to honor a teammate and friend whose passion for robotics inspired us all.

2019 Season: Deep Space

2019
Mechanical Leadership

In my sophomore year, I stepped into the role of Mechanical Lead for our Deep Space robot. The game required manipulating polycarbonate disks and large yoga balls, then climbing a 2-foot platform during endgame.

Our solution featured multiple intakes: low-positioned for rapid ground collection, and arm-mounted for precise high-scoring. This versatility made us effective in various gameplay situations. I also designed our climbing mechanism that could ascend the platform in just 7 seconds.

Mid-season, I redesigned a critical game piece handler using only a side photo of the robot (as FRC rules prohibited modifications during competition). This impromptu redesign significantly improved our performance at districts.

Off-Season Project: Brace-Free Elevator

PROJECT
Engineering Innovation

During an off-season, I tackled the challenge of creating an elevator system that wouldn't require top bracing, which would interfere with pivoting arms transferring game pieces. My solution was to fully constrain the uprights so they became their own support as the elevator extended.

This project became a deep dive into manufacturing and CNC machining. With my teacher only familiar with SheetCam for 2D parts, I taught myself Fusion 360 to generate 3D g-code. I started by designing a modular bracket that could be used throughout the system, precisely toleranced to hold bearings at the perfect spacing around aluminum extrusions.

After prototyping in foam, I manufactured final components from HDPE. Though never implemented on a competition robot, this project significantly advanced my CAD, CAM, and manufacturing skills while demonstrating innovative approaches to mechanical constraints.

Technical Approach

The elevator was designed around the principle of self-supporting uprights that gain stability as they extend. Each section interlocks with the next, creating a rigid structure without external bracing.

I created a universal bracket design that could be used at every joint point. This required precise tolerancing (±0.005") to ensure smooth operation while maintaining structural integrity. Bearings were positioned to perfectly align with the aluminum extrusions, minimizing friction while preventing binding.

The project was a comprehensive learning experience in designing for manufacturability, CNC programming, and precision assembly. Though not competition-tested, it demonstrated innovative solutions to common robotics challenges.
