First Robotics Competition

Team Captain & Mechanical Lead | 2017-2021

Overview

FIRST Robotics Competition (FRC) challenges high school students to design, build, and program industrial-sized robots to compete in challenging field games. As Team Captain and Mechanical Lead throughout high school, I led multidisciplinary teams in solving complex engineering problems under strict deadlines, developing leadership, technical, and problem-solving skills.

My responsibilities included mechanical design leadership, project management, strategic decision-making, and mentoring younger team members. This experience fostered my passion for robotics and engineering while demonstrating my ability to lead technical teams to success.

2020 Season: Infinite Recharge

As Team Captain in my junior year, I led the design of a robot to shoot dodgeballs across the field. After observing early competitions, I initiated and executed a complete redesign in one week. Although the season was canceled due to COVID-19, this experience demonstrated rapid adaptation and leadership under pressure.

Our initial approach focused on a tank drive with a 360° turret to maximize scoring potential. The design prioritized a low profile to navigate under field obstacles, but this created challenges for feeding balls into the turret.

To ensure consistent shooting regardless of turret orientation, I developed a central feeding mechanism using a piston instead of a ramp. The height constraints required a custom piston extender that doubled the throw distance - a solution achieved with standard components to avoid exceeding size limitations.

The turret featured a flywheel with vertical adjustment hood, precision machined to maintain consistent energy transfer during shooting. Careful wire management prevented interference with moving components.

After observing the first competition, I realized our original design wouldn't be competitive. With seniors away on trips, I proposed a complete redesign that the team enthusiastically supported.

The new "washing machine" design featured a carousel spinner with a hollow center tube. An omni-wheel positioned above a cutout in the tube allowed balls to pass when stationary, but when activated, it pushed balls into the center tube and toward the flywheel.

This innovative solution dramatically improved our scoring capacity - from one ball every 3-4 seconds to five balls (the maximum allowed) in the same timeframe. The redesign was completed in just one week, demonstrating rapid iteration and problem-solving capabilities.

In Memory of Sam Ozer

Sam Ozer was a brilliant and beloved member of our team who tragically passed away in a biking accident during the off-season. His creativity and passion for robotics inspired everyone who worked with him.

To honor Sam's legacy, I completed work on his ingenious climbing mechanism concept that he was developing before his passing.

Sam's innovative approach used two opposed tape measures to deploy a climbing hook with Kevlar rope. The opposing orientation prevented buckling that would occur with a single tape measure.

I transformed Sam's laser-cut prototypes into a durable metal mechanism capable of handling competition forces. The final design was integrated into our robot as a tribute to his creativity and dedication.

Completing this project was both a technical challenge and a meaningful way to honor our teammate's contribution to the team and the robotics community.

2019 Season: Destination Deep Space

As Mechanical Lead in my sophomore year, I designed a robot to manipulate polycarbonate disks and large yoga balls, with the ability to climb a 2-foot platform during endgame. Our versatile design featured multiple intake systems at different heights for optimal scoring.

During a district competition, I identified an opportunity to improve our game piece handling. Using only a photo of the robot's side, I designed a new component that we added during the competition (within FRC's bagging rules), significantly enhancing functionality.

Our climbing mechanism proved exceptionally effective, enabling the robot to ascend the platform in just 7 seconds. This capability gave us a strategic advantage during endgame scoring.

In the off-season Ramp Riot event, our robot's performance earned us first place. This victory demonstrated the durability and effectiveness of our design beyond the regular competition season.

2018 Season: Power Up

My rookie season introduced me to competitive robotics through a game requiring robots to manipulate milk crate-sized boxes. Our reverse four-bar parallel lift earned us the Excellence in Engineering Award and qualification for the World Championship in Detroit.

This foundational experience taught me team dynamics, engineering principles, and competition strategy. Competing at the highest level as a freshman was both challenging and transformative, setting the stage for my future leadership roles.

Off-Season Project: Top-Braceless Elevator

To enable future robots with pivoting arms that could transfer game pieces across the robot, I developed an elevator system that wouldn't require top bracing. This constraint led to an innovative solution where the uprights become self-bracing as the elevator extends.

I designed a modular bracket system that could be precisely replicated for consistent performance. Each bracket was engineered to hold bearings at exact tolerances to smoothly guide the elevator along aluminum extrusions.

With our teacher only familiar with 2D machining (SheetCAM), I taught myself Fusion 360 to create 3D models and generate G-code for CNC machining. This expanded our team's manufacturing capabilities beyond previous limitations.

The development process included iterative prototyping - first in foam for rapid testing, then in HDPE for functional validation. This approach allowed refinement before committing to final materials.

Although this specific elevator wasn't implemented in competition, the skills gained in CAD, CNC programming, and precision manufacturing have proven invaluable in subsequent engineering projects.
